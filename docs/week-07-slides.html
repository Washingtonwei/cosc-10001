Your data travels in pieces,
through strangers' machines

  • A page is chopped into packets
  • Each one hops through a dozen or more machines you didn't choose
  • Any of them can read anything that isn't encrypted

Hold that thought. That's next week.

3 of 3

Distance is time

  • Nothing beats light, and in glass fibre light does about 200,000 km per second
  • A geosynchronous satellite is 22,000 miles up
  • Up and down again: about a quarter of a second, before anything else happens

Money can't make light faster.
Hold on to that sentence.

The demo

Let's check
Howard's route.

Three free commands, already on your laptop.

Check 1 · where is TCU's website?
nslookup www.tcu.edu
The answer ends in

us-east-1.
amazonaws.com

TCU's website doesn't live at TCU. It lives in an Amazon data center in Northern Virginia, about 1,200 miles away.

Check 2 · how long is the round trip?
ping -c 4 www.tcu.edu

On Windows, leave off the -c 4.

Request timeout

Is TCU's website down?

No. Amazon just doesn't answer pings.

A failed command tells you something too.
Just not what you'd guess.

Check 3 · near versus far
ping -c 4 1.1.1.1
ping -c 4 www.anu.edu.au

A server a few miles away, then the Australian National University in Canberra.

Check 4 · every hop on the way
traceroute -q 1 -w 1 www.anu.edu.au

Windows: tracert www.anu.edu.au. The hop names say where they are.

What we just saw
Dallas~7 ms
Los Angeles~37 ms
Sydney · the bottom of the Pacific~170 ms
Canberra~188 ms

LA to Sydney is about 12,000 km. At 200,000 km per second that's 60 ms there and 60 back: 120 ms.

We just measured the speed of light
from a classroom in Fort Worth.
