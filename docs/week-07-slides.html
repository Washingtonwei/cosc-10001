Your data travels in pieces,
through strangers' machines

  • A page is chopped into packets
  • Each one hops through a dozen or more machines you didn't choose
  • Any of them can read anything that isn't encrypted

Hold that thought. That's next week.

So how does a packet know where to go?

It doesn't.

Nobody on the way knows the whole route. Every router knows one thing: which of its neighbours is closer to the address.

Like road signs. No sign knows the way from Fort Worth to Canberra. Each one just points "Australia: this way."

One packet, hop by hop

Look up the address. Pick a neighbour. Pass it on. Forget it.

A real one has about a million rows, and it picks one for every packet in a few billionths of a second.

Who writes the signs?

Networks announce the addresses they can reach, and every router passes it on. It's called BGP.

Distance is time

  • Nothing beats light, and in glass fibre light does about 200,000 km per second
  • A geosynchronous satellite is 22,000 miles up
  • Up and down again: about a quarter of a second, before anything else happens

Money can't make light faster.
Hold on to that sentence.

How traceroute sees the hops

Every packet carries a hop limit. Each router takes one off. Whoever hits zero throws it away and says so, and that reply gives away where it is.

Check 4 · every hop on the way
traceroute -q 1 -w 1 www.anu.edu.au

Windows: tracert www.anu.edu.au. The hop names say where they are.

What we just saw
Dallas~7 ms
Los Angeles~37 ms
Sydney · the bottom of the Pacific~170 ms
Canberra~188 ms

LA to Sydney is about 12,000 km. At 200,000 km per second that's 60 ms there and 60 back: 120 ms.
